# L-142 — A rule failing a correct artifact in bulk is evidence about the rule or about what its check looks at, and from inside the failing artifact the two read identically

Forty of forty-six labels fail one rule. Every attempt to fix them makes the slide worse. From
inside that deck there is exactly one available reading: **the rule is wrong**. It is a good
reading, it is often right, and here it was not.

Found 2026-08-29 closing [T-256](../../tasks/T-256-ds-219-cannot-see-a-painted-svg-ancestor.md),
from ClaimAI adopter report `019`. DS-219 asks a labelled mark for two numbers — the mark against
its ground, the text against the mark. The numbers were the right two. What was wrong was **the
surface the first one was taken against**: the ground walk stopped at the nearest CSS background and
could not see a painted panel *inside* the figure, so a pale card resting on a dark panel was
measured against the page. The design's contrast was real and the instrument was looking past it.

The report is careful and it is not at fault: it names the mechanism correctly — *it stops at the
mark* — and then draws the wider conclusion, because the deck could not tell the two apart. It also
found this repository's own recorded doubt about the rule and offered it in support, which made the
wider reading look confirmed by both sides.

**The tell is which way the failures point.** A rule that has outrun its argument fails artifacts
that differ from each other; an instrument looking at the wrong thing fails everything sharing one
structural feature. Forty-six labels in one deck is one feature, not forty-six judgements.

**How to apply.**

1. **Before amending a rule that fails in bulk, reproduce one failure and print what the check
   actually measured** — not its verdict, its inputs. Here that is one number: the colour it took as
   the ground.
2. **Ask what the failing cases share.** If the answer is a structure rather than a design choice,
   suspect the instrument. Bulk uniformity is a property of code, not of taste.
3. **A repository's own recorded doubt is not corroboration.** It was written about a different
   question and it will fit any story you bring it. Read what it settled, and when.
4. **Fix the instrument first, then re-measure, then decide about the rule.** In this order the
   decision is cheap: if the rule is still wrong, it now fails a smaller and more interesting set.
5. **Separate *unmeasurable* from *failing* while you are in there.** Both defects here were the
   same shape — a check taking a number it had no business taking, and reporting the result as a
   verdict. A pair with no ground to read is not a pair that failed, and telling an author to fix a
   contrast nothing measured is how a rule loses its reader ([L-36](L-36.md)).
