# L-102 — Verifying the part you were worried about proves nothing about the part you broke

Met 2026-08-15 (**T-157**, found by its recipient). A register of observations was transformed on the
way out: links made absolute, and the sender-facing status banner replaced with one written for the
reader. The worry was obvious and correct — **the rows must not change** — so the delivery was
verified by comparing them:

```
handoff-skill rows 7 identical True | audit 3 implementation 4 | priority-words 0
```

**The rows were fine. The damage was two paragraphs further down.** The banner replacement matched
*every* blockquote in the document, not the first one, so a quotation of the rule the report was
proposing to change became a second copy of the report's own banner. The recipient found it by
reading. Nothing here could have, because the check was built out of the hypothesis instead of out of
the change.

**The shape: a targeted verification is only as good as the guess that aimed it.** It answers *did the
thing I feared happen*, which is a different question from *what did I actually change*. The first
question is cheap and reassuring and was answered correctly; the second was never asked. A transform
narrow in intent — "replace the banner" — was broad in implementation, and intent is not what runs.

**So diff the whole artifact against its source, then explain every difference.** Not the part under
suspicion: all of it. Three differences were expected here — banner, two link forms — and a fourth
would have shown up immediately in a diff of maybe fifteen lines. The cost of the complete comparison
was lower than the cost of the clever one.

**The tell:** you wrote an assertion into the transform (`assert "../" not in text`) and a comparison
after it, and both test properties you named in advance. Nothing in the procedure looks at what the
tool did that you did not ask about.
